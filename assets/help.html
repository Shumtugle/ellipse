Help

How Ellipse works, part by part. Almost everything is reached in two ways: by holding a thing on the home screen, or from the settings, which open from the home screen's own menu.

THE HOME SCREEN

The screens stand side by side; swipe across to turn them. One of them is the home screen, ringed among the points at the foot; the Home button brings it back. Hold an empty place for the home screen's menu: add a screen, a widget, a shortcut, a folder, make this screen the home one, remove this screen — always asked first, empty or not — or open the settings.

With endless scrolling on, the last screen is followed by the first, as if the screens were a ring.

Picture of the home screen, in the same menu, makes a picture to show others: the screen on its wallpaper in a card, beside the date and a swatch of the wallpaper. It is saved among your pictures and offered to be sent.

ICONS, FOLDERS AND WIDGETS

Holding a thing opens its menu: an app's own shortcuts first, then what the home screen can do to it — change its icon, rename it, put it in a category, uninstall it, resize it, remove it. A name given to an app, a shortcut or a link is shown wherever it stands, and the list still finds the app by its own name too; clear the name, and its own comes back. Keep the finger moving instead, and the thing is carried: set it down anywhere free, on another screen, in the dock, or on a folder to put it in.

Lying down, as the phone turns, every upright screen is seen whole on a screen of its own, in three parts — one, one and a half. The first part is the upright screen's top and foot put together: its rows with widgets as they stand, and the dock under them when the half part is needed; the second holds its rows of icons, still rows; the half part takes the rows left over, each turned into a column, the lowest row nearest the right edge. When no screen needs the half part, the dock stands there as a column. On a narrower screen everything is drawn a little smaller so the three parts fit. Lying down is a view of the upright set-out: things are added and moved upright; a thing's menu lying down — taking away, resizing — works on it upright. Only the home screen turns: the settings, the pages — the weather, the state, colour and text, this help — and the card that pins a widget or a shortcut always stand upright, and the night clock always lies on its side.

Own status strip, under Desktop: while the phone's status bar is hidden, the home screen may stand a strip of its own in its place, as tall as it is — signs: the signal, Wi-Fi, a bell with how many apps have something to say, the date, the charge — each of which can be left out. On a screen without the home screen's clock the strip tells the time and the date in its middle; where the clock stands, it leaves them to the clock. Steps of the signal and of Wi-Fi not reached are the same ink, see-through: ghosts of the lit ones. A swipe down from its left half lowers the notifications, from its right half the quick settings, and a touch on a sign opens what it tells of. While the phone charges, a bolt stands across the battery. Its signs are dark on a light wallpaper and light on a dark one — measured at the very top of the wallpaper where the home screen drew or keeps it, as the phone's own bar judges it otherwise — each with a soft shade under it so a mid-tone never swallows it, or wear the accent or a colour of your own; it steps aside with the rest of the home screen every few minutes so an OLED screen keeps no line, asks for no leave, and steps aside when the phone's own bar comes back for a moment.

Show the wallpaper, in the home screen's menu, takes the screens and the dock softly away and raises a sheet at the foot, on the wallpaper itself blurred and darkened where the home screen can know it: what is on the wall now, and two plain ways to change it — Change in the home screen's settings, which opens the wallpaper room, and Change in the phone's settings, which opens the phone's own chooser. A touch anywhere else brings the home screen back. And the whole home screen steps a few points aside every three minutes and each time it comes back, too slowly to see, so an OLED screen keeps no line burnt in; it can be turned off under Look.

A copy file opened from anywhere — the phone's files, a message, a mail — comes to Ellipse by itself: it shows its picture and asks before it is brought back. Copies made from this version on are known by their own kind; an older one is brought back from the settings, Backup and restore.

Copies carry a small picture of the home screen as it stood — screens, dock and, where it can be known, the wallpaper — and show it before they come back, so it is seen what is being brought back.

State is a page as the weather's, about the phone itself: three rings at its head, as the clock's are drawn — the charge outside, the free memory inside it, the free storage innermost — with whether it charges, the battery's health (in percent where the phone tells it) and, from Android 14, how many times it has been filled; its warmth and volts, the free working memory and storage, the last security update and system update, the Android version and how long the phone has run. The day as the home screen counts it itself stands there too: how long the screen has been on today, the charge the day began with and has now, the warmest the battery has been. A touch on the charge or the memory on the clock opens it; a touch on the battery's ring there opens what used the battery. Nothing of it asks for any leave.

Arrange bubbles, in the clock's menu on the home screen, lets each bubble be dragged to its place and sized by the knob on its edge, or by two fingers; a double touch sends one back into the chain.

Six more clocks, each the time, the weather and the charge in a shape of its own: Eclipse, two circles overlapping, the time in one, the weather in the other, the charge in the lens between; Horizon, the sky above with the weather in it, coloured by the hour and the rain, the charge as the line of the horizon, the time below; Reservoir, a capsule filling at its right like a tank; Flip, two flip cards whose top leaf falls as the minute turns, the town, the temperature with the day's high and low, and the charge under them — a homage to a clock a well-loved phone once wore; Monogram, the time across the whole face, the minutes in the accent; Three stones, the time, the weather and the charge each on a stone of its own, cut from the clock's material. On every clock a touch on the time, digits or hands, opens the clock, and a touch on the date opens the calendar; the weather and the charge open their own pages. Each may stand on a dark ground, on glass or on nothing; what shows the charge — the lens, the horizon, the tank, the minutes — takes the accent, the charge's own colour from green to red, or white; the flip cards are dark, light, or cut from the clock's material. The headphones' charge shows on them too, while they are near.

Night clock, in the home screen's menu last before the settings (and on a gesture, if you like), lays the clock across the whole screen on its side, darker than by day. Half a minute untouched, it goes down into full black, and a touch brings it back; a double touch, or anything done with two fingers, closes it. The face drifts a few points now and then, so the screen keeps no line. In its settings: which face; how much darker; how long the screen is kept on — a quarter of an hour, half an hour or an hour, after which the phone sleeps as always; whether it goes into the dark; quiet while it is open, alarms still ringing; and its hours — within them, with the phone on the charger if you ask it so, the home screen opens the night clock by itself, once a night, and it closes when they are over.

The home screen's menu is in two parts: above, what adds to the screens; below a hairline and always in sight, what is wanted at hand — the picture of the home screen, the night clock, the bare wallpaper and the settings.

Grey, a deed for any gesture — the pinch has it unless you gave the pinch something else — takes the colour off the home screen and gives it back: the screens, the dock, the list of every app. The apps themselves keep their colours; the whole phone goes grey by the phone's own settings, where it has them.

A new ground, among the home screen's own under Ellipse in Add widget, is a die on a screen: a touch throws a new wallpaper, from where the settings say grounds come from, and the die comes up with another face — one to six pips, now and then seven, and once in a long while the sign of the endless.

Add shortcut, in the home screen's menu, holds three kinds: the shortcuts apps make; Apps, any app's own icon, set down in the first free place; and Runs — an app's other ways in. Choose the app, then one of the screens it lets others open by name, each under its name and, faint, its class; the search finds either. A run opens that screen directly. If the app later closes that way in, the run says so; if the app is gone, the run waits faint in its place under the name it was kept with.

Folders are made from the home screen's menu, or by setting an app down on a folder. A large folder shows its apps as a small page of its own; resize it from its menu. Change icon in a folder's menu lays out its small icons its own way — four, nine, a ring, a stack, a fan, a tower — or puts a picture of your own, a symbol or a mark in its face; a folder holding widgets shows the icons of their apps. Marks: under Picture, From the dice throws a page of pictograms made the way things are made — slabs side by side, a ring with a handle, a roof on walls, rays round a middle, rings in a chain, a head on shoulders — each shown on the icon's own plate. A third of the throws are beings: a body — a cloud, a circle, a heart, a drop, a gem, a peak, a box — and what grows on it, ears, legs, a trunk, a tail, a beak, rain, a bolt, eyes, a beat, joined into one outline; the eye finds trees, cats, sheep, birds, jellyfish and stranger things. Touch one to put it on; hold one for more like it; the dice below throws again. Marks you have put on are kept under My marks, to be taken again anywhere. Hold one of them for more like it, or to forget it; where it stands on an icon already, it stays. A widget can be set down on a folder too: it stands above the folder's apps, as wide as its card and as tall as it stood; hold it for its menu, or keep the finger moving to carry it out.

Widgets come from the widget shelf. The phone asks once whether Ellipse may make widgets; allow it always, and widgets are placed without asking again. A widget is resized from its menu, and may wear a frame of the icons' materials. Stacks: Stack in a widget's menu opens its card, where more widgets are added from the shelf to stand in the same place. A sideways swipe that begins on a stack turns it, round and round; short lines along its lower edge tell how many it holds and which is shown. In the card, carry a widget by its lines to change the order — the first is shown first — take one out by its cross, and let the stack turn by itself now and then if you like. With one widget left, it is a plain widget again.

Half steps let a thing stand half a place along or down. Overlap lets things lie over one another; the last set down is on top, and a thing's menu can bring it to the front or send it behind.

THE DOCK

A bar of four at the foot of every screen, and the way into every app. Carry an app onto it to put it there, or off it to take it away. It can be hidden in the Desktop settings.

ALL APPS

Every app, in lines going down or in pages going across. The bar at the foot searches as you write; its menu sorts the list — from A to Z, the newest first, or the most lately updated. Hold an app to carry it onto a screen.

Categories put tabs across the list: none; kinds you name and fill yourself, from an app's menu; or the kinds the apps say they are. An app you move yourself stays where you put it. The list's background may be the theme's own or any colour, bright or dim, solid or clear.

Hidden apps are left out of the list and its search; they are chosen in the settings.

WHAT IS FRESH

The Back gesture on the home screen lowers a panel with three tabs: the apps opened most from here, those opened last, and those installed or updated lately — each with a word saying which. Push it back up to close it.

THE CLOCK

The home screen's own clock is a thing of the grid: hold it to resize it or move it. It has ten faces — the first, in the colours of the theme; the plate; the glass card; the bubbles; and six of other shapes: eclipse, horizon, reservoir, flip, monogram and three stones — each with its own settings. On every face a touch on the time opens the clock, a touch on the date the calendar, and the weather and the charge their own pages.

The bubbles are glass circles: a big one with the hour, and up to seven small ones, each holding what you choose — the time, the weather, the calendar, the charge, how warm it feels, the humidity, the wind, the daylight, the rain, the next alarm, the free memory, the sun's ultraviolet, the air's pressure, or the player. The player's bubble pauses or goes on with whatever plays at a touch; while the home screen may read notifications, it also shows what plays and fills as far as it has played. A bubble for the headphones joins them while they are connected.

THE WEATHER

A tap on the weather opens its page: the sky now and in the hours to come, the days ahead, the wind, the air, pollen, the sun, and the chance of an aurora. The place is chosen in the settings, or is where the phone roughly is.

THE LOOK

Theme: dark, light, or as the phone is set. Colour and text: the accent, the ground, how solid the cards are. Icons: the outline every icon is cut to, from circles to the paper tile, the chrome plate and the sticker; the rim, glass and gloss of the materials; the size of icons and their names; packs of icons, whose own pictures, or whose ground, mask and gloss, the icons take. Typeface: the letters of every name and every word.

Wallpaper, a room of the settings' first page, opens on three tiles. The phone's wallpapers opens the phone's own chooser — its wallpaper app, live wallpapers, whatever else offers one; Framed by the phone lets you choose a picture and hands it to the phone's own framing, which crops it and asks for the home screen, the lock screen or both. Set this way, the wallpaper is the phone's: a copy carries it only with leave to read all files. Kept by Ellipse keeps the chosen picture in Ellipse itself, so it always travels with a copy. And the room draws a ground of its own, in layers: a light — a curtain of colour, a horizon, a wash; a texture — cloth, plaster, gravel, grooves, wood, aerated concrete, sand, brushed metal, a biome, ripples; an ornament — circles, branches, drops on glass, stars; and the edges darkened. Each has its colour, strength and scale. The ready grounds are good combinations; the dice throw new ones, and the arrows go back to those thrown before. Done sets it on the home screen, and on the lock screen too unless you keep that one as it is; a ground you like can be kept under a name. And a new ground can come by itself while the phone sleeps: after the screen has been dark for the time you choose, one is drawn — by the dice, from the ready ones or from your own — so the phone wakes to it. Turning it on asks first if the wallpaper now was not set here; under it, a line tells what the last night did.

Your picture: the phone does not let a home screen read the wallpaper it has, so a picture of your own is brought in instead, with Wallpaper from a picture. Ellipse keeps it, turned upright and sized for the screen, sets it, and carries it in copies; Your picture again puts it back after any ground. Before a ground replaces a wallpaper Ellipse did not set, it asks once; and a new ground never comes in the phone's sleep over a wallpaper that was not let go of.

Random, the second of the presets, lets the dice dress the home screen under a few rules of what belongs together — one material for the icons' rims, the clock's plate and the widgets' frames, an accent that belongs to that material, windows with symbols only in the heavier materials; the size of the words, the grids and what stands on the screens it never touches. Like every preset it is only tried on until Accept the settings, or the round button in the bar. A random look kept a minute and more is remembered among the Lucky random looks, out of the way under one line: twenty at most, the oldest going by themselves.

Folders in the list: held long in the list of every app, an app goes Into a folder… — one of the list's own, or a new one. The folder stands among the apps as they do, by its name when the list goes by name, and its apps leave the list; a touch opens its card, the same card a folder of the screens opens into, and an app is taken out of it there. Held, the folder is renamed or ungrouped, its apps back in the list. The list's search finds the apps inside the folders too. The list's folders hold apps only, no widgets.

Materials for rims, frames and the clock: metal, gold, wood, steel in the finest brushed grain, black, glass, the accent, silk in pink, nude, gold and silver, and mesh, dense as a stocking's, dark or light. Embossing belongs inside shapes — the icons' plates and a dial — and is not offered for frames or the clock's case; the sequins have left the stage, and a rim once made of them is drawn in gold silk.

Presets are tried on before they are taken: a touch on one shows it in the sample at the head of the room — the clock, icons and a folder, and a piece of the home screen's menu, where the accent shows best — with what it is under the tiles. Accept the settings, or the round button in the bar, takes it; going back, or leaving the settings, puts the look before on again, before the home screen shows. Material 3 puts it back as it first came: icons in the phone's own shape and colours, the first clock, no rims or frames. Random, Steel, Meno, Bubbles, Paper, Walnut, Gold and Noir each dress it whole: the icons' outline and rim, the clock and its case, the widgets' frames, the accent. Your own can be kept under a name. A preset never touches what you set out yourself — the grids, the apps and their places, the size of the words, the names under the icons — and the look worn before can be put back. Every room that shows something keeps its sample at the one size, sixteen by nine; choices shown as pictures stand in an even grid, all in sight.

THE GRID

The Desktop settings choose the columns and rows, the shape of a place — the screen's share, square, wide or tall — the height of the rows, and how the grid meets the screen's edges: within its margins, widgets past them, or no margins at all. With no margins at all, an icon may fill its place whole: at the largest size the icons meet one another and, in the outer columns, the screen's edge, every one still at the middle of its place. Past them, the clock's plate and the Meno face come to touch the screen's own edge too, as a framed widget does; the first clock keeps its margins. The phone's status bar and navigation bar may be hidden over the home screen; a swipe from the edge shows them for a moment.

GESTURES

A swipe up or down, two fingers swept up or down, a pinch, a double tap on the empty home screen, the Back gesture and the Home button can each be given anything: nothing, all apps, the search for apps, the notifications, the quick settings, what is fresh, the home screen, locking the phone, these settings, or any app you choose.

The phone is locked in one of two ways. Through an accessibility service it locks at once; Ellipse reads nothing on the screen, but some apps, a bank's for one, refuse to open while such a service from outside the phone's store is on. As a keeper of the phone, allowed to lock it and nothing more, those apps do not mind — only the first unlock after may ask for the code rather than a finger.

BACKUPS

Back up into a file keeps every screen, the dock, the folders, the clock, every setting and every kept look — with a language brought from a file and the pictures icons were given — in one file, saved wherever you choose. With Keep the wallpaper in the copy on, the pictures of the home screen and the lock screen go in too; the phone shows them only to an app allowed to read all files, and Ellipse asks for that only then. Send a copy hands the same file to whatever the phone sends with — a cloud, a mail, a chat. Restore from a file brings it back; what was there is first set aside, so the restore can be undone. Once a day, at the first start of the day, the set-out and every setting are copied aside by themselves — one copy, today's.

From another home screen: the same Restore takes the backups of some other home screens and brings in their screens, apps at their places, folders, shortcuts and links to pages or contacts, widgets where the phone allows, their dock, the apps they hid where the copy says so plainly (otherwise which apps to hide is chosen here, by hand), their kinds of apps as categories, their hidden bars, their wallpaper where the copy keeps it, the names and icon pictures their owner gave single apps, and much of how they looked. Apps that are not on this phone wait grey in their places under their names: a touch finds them in the store, and once installed they stand there themselves. The dress of this home screen is let go of first, so the look brought in is not laid over an old one. Widgets the phone does not let Ellipse make at once are named, to be added again.

Widgets belong to the phone. A copy remembers whose each widget is; when it is restored on a phone that no longer holds them — after Ellipse is installed anew, or on another phone — they are made again in their places, if Ellipse is allowed to make widgets always. Those the phone asks about first are named, to be added again from the shelf.

LANGUAGES

English, Finnish and Russian come with Ellipse. Any other language can be made: save the English template, put every phrase after = into the language, by hand or with an assistant, and bring the file in. A phrase a module lacks is said in English.

WHEN SOMETHING IS WRONG

Last error keeps where and why the home screen last stopped; copy it and send it as words. Restart launcher closes the home screen and opens it again. Reset launcher forgets everything set by hand — make a backup first. If a version breaks something, the copy it kept of the version before is in Backup and restore.
